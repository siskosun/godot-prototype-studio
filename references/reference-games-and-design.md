# Current reference games and complete prototype design

Use for new game ideas and meaningful mechanic redesigns, not typo fixes, isolated bugs, or already-settled polish. The purpose is to improve a concrete design decision, not produce a market report.

## Find the closest released comparisons

Extract the player's verbs, loop, information available at decisions, failure/recovery, session structure, social relationship, and controls. Search by those mechanics as well as genre. Include older close matches and a current-release search; newest does not mean closest.

Use official store listings, developer pages, manuals, update notes, and gameplay footage. Verify the exact game and platform, release status, available version or date, and actual mechanics before using them. Separate released products, Early Access, public demos, unreleased announcements, and unavailable titles. A launch planned in the past is not proof of release. Demos and announcements can be secondary inspiration, not the requested released-game baseline.

Usually one nearest reference and up to two complementary references suffice. Expand only while a search could materially change the chosen mechanic, differentiation, or feasibility. Do not claim an exhaustive worldwide nearest match; say 'closest among the verified references'. If browsing is unavailable, try other available authorized sources, disclose the gap, and do not fabricate current status. Current comparison research is a default design aid, not a completion prerequisite. Its absence blocks DONE only when the brief explicitly makes current market/reference research part of the deliverable, or when a rights/feasibility fact must be resolved before safe implementation. Otherwise continue from the user's brief and verified project evidence.

## Compare design, not popularity

For each useful reference record the actual player action and decision, what works for the proposed audience/input, the likely weakness or cost, and the specific adaptation. Cite observations; label design advantages and disadvantages as judgments with their assumptions. Do not infer retention, sales, fun, or market demand from store prominence or one review.

Compare only dimensions that matter: immediate readability, meaningful choices, pacing, skill versus randomness, failure cost, social interaction, content burden, control suitability, or visual communication. Avoid invented weighted scores.

End with one recommended improvement: what to retain, what to change, why that changes play rather than just the theme, and what could show the change made the game worse. Borrow mechanics and general principles, not proprietary characters, art, audio, names, code, or level expression.

## Turn the idea into a buildable whole

Use `templates/design_blueprint.md` when a new prototype needs a durable design. Fill only relevant parts, but leave no missing rule that would prevent one complete session:

- Player promise, audience, controls, viewpoint, and intended session.
- Core loop; valid and invalid actions; information shown before decisions.
- Mechanic transitions: trigger, precondition, action, state change, feedback, ending.
- Win/loss or sandbox session completion, recovery/restart, onboarding, and state continuity.
- Small representative content set, progression within the session, tunable starting values, and interactions among systems.
- Original visual/audio direction, UI states, feedback hierarchy, required effects, and accessibility needs.
- Implementation scope and excluded expansion; reference comparison and design uncertainty.

Do not add combat, economy, meta progression, networking, or accounts merely to fill headings. For a sandbox or toy, define an observable creation/use/reset loop instead of inventing win/loss. Numerical tuning is a starting design choice, not empirical balance evidence.

Ensure each recurring option has a plausible useful context. Distinguish replacements from stackable upgrades; make costs, synergies, and exclusions explicit when a build/crafting system exists. First-session content must demonstrate the differentiating mechanic, not just a tutorial that ends before it appears.

For 'one idea to a finished prototype', deliver this design *and implement it*. A comparison table, GDD, foundation scene, or list of future features is not the final result. Do not stop to ask which recommended reversible variant to build when the user delegated that choice.
